# Hook

A thousand years before anyone knew what electricity was, sailors were trusting their lives to a sliver of magic: a magnetised needle, floated on water or balanced on a pin, that *always knew north* — across fog, through starless nights, in seas no chart described. Nobody could say why. The needle simply knew, and empires followed it.

The needle was reading a field — an invisible architecture of influence filling all the space around every magnet, including the colossal one you're standing on: planet Earth, whose molten iron heart churns out a magnetic field that shields you from solar wind and steers compasses, sea turtles, and racing pigeons alike. Magnetism is the module's second invisible river, and this lesson maps its geography: poles, fields, the chosen materials that answer it — and the strange, unbroken rule that its poles refuse, always and everywhere, to travel alone.

# Core Learning

## Concept Introduction

**Poles — the rules of engagement.** Every magnet has a **north pole** and a **south pole** (named for which way each end points when swung freely). The interaction law mirrors charge:

- **Like poles repel; unlike attract** — with force fading rapidly with distance.
- **But unlike charge: poles never come alone.** Cut a magnet and you get two complete magnets; cut forever, same story, down to atoms. The isolated monopole has never been found — a clue (kept for tomorrow) that magnetism is made of something *circulating*, not something you can bottle by halves.

**Fields — the influence mapped.** A magnetic field fills the space around a magnet; **field lines** draw it:

- Run **N → S outside** the magnet (closing S → N inside — every line is a loop)
- **Denser = stronger** (crowded at poles, where paperclips leap)
- **Never cross** (a compass at one point can point only one way)
- A small compass *is* the field-probe: its needle settles along the local line. The filings are a thousand compasses at once.

**Materials — the chosen few.** Strong response (**ferromagnetism**) belongs to **iron, nickel, cobalt** and their alloys (steel) alone among common materials — their atoms are themselves tiny magnets that can align in regions called **domains**. Magnetising = aligning domains (stroke steel with a magnet; or, tomorrow, use a current); demagnetising = scrambling them (heat, hammering). Everything else — aluminium, copper, plastic, you — is effectively transparent to a magnet's pull. *Conductor* and *magnetic* are different clubs: copper conducts beautifully and ignores magnets; steel does both; plastic, neither.

**Earth — the magnet underfoot.** The planet's outer core — molten iron, churning with heat and rotation — generates a planet-scale field (a *dynamo*, in a sense tomorrow will deepen). Field lines emerge near one geographic pole, sweep the globe, and dive near the other; a compass needle aligns with them. Quirks worth knowing: the magnetic poles wander (and have *flipped* many times in geological history, recorded in seafloor rock); and the field is our shield — deflecting solar-wind particles into the auroras at the poles rather than into our atmosphere everywhere.

## Why It Matters

- Magnetism runs the recycling line (steel sorted from aluminium), the fridge door, the hard drive, the MRI scanner — and, after tomorrow's lesson, every motor and generator on Earth.
- Field-line literacy is the visual language for the rest of this module — currents' fields, motors' geometry, and induction all speak it.
- Earth's field is navigation's oldest instrument and geology's tape recorder; its physics connects compasses, auroras, and the planet's deep interior.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: The recycling line's silent sorter**
A conveyor of mixed cans passes under a powerful magnet: steel cans leap upward and ride the magnetised belt away; aluminium cans trundle on unmoved. One field, one materials fact, millions of tonnes sorted annually. (The aluminium gets its own cleverer trick — eddy currents — which needs tomorrow's physics; magnetism's story compounds.)

**Example 2: Reading a field map like a navigator**
Two bar magnets placed N-facing-N: the filing pattern shows lines bowing *away* from each other, a thin empty corridor between the poles — the repulsion zone, drawn. Place N-facing-S instead: lines stream uninterrupted from one to the other, a dense bridge — attraction's portrait. The patterns aren't illustrations of the force; properly read, they *are* the force diagram.

**Example 3: The compass that lies a little**
A hiker's compass in Britain points ~1° west of true north; in parts of Canada, 20°+ off. The needle faithfully follows *magnetic* field lines, which converge on the wandering magnetic pole, not the geographic one. Map margins print the local correction (declination) and its annual drift — navigation has run on "trust the needle, then correct it" for five centuries. Pigeons, turtles, and migratory birds appear to read the same field, corrections included, with senses we're still reverse-engineering.

## Common Mistakes

- **"Magnets attract all metals"** — iron/nickel/cobalt (and alloys) only; aluminium and copper shrug. Test, don't assume.
- **Expecting isolated poles** — every fragment is a complete dipole; "north piece and south piece" has never once been produced.
- **Reading field lines as real threads** — they're a map convention (you could draw twice as many); density *ratios* and directions are the physics.
- **Confusing magnetic with geographic north** — they differ by a wandering correction; and (delicious pedantry) Earth's *magnetic-south-type* pole currently sits up in the Arctic — that's exactly why compass *north* poles point there.
- **Thinking Earth's field comes from a buried bar magnet** — solid iron at core temperatures can't hold magnetisation; it's the *motion* of molten conducting iron (a dynamo — tomorrow's physics, planet-sized).

## Mental Model

A magnetic field is **a silent current of influence, like wind made visible only by what it moves**. The filings are autumn leaves: toss them into the invisible wind and they align along its streamlines, crowding where it blows hardest. Every magnet is a self-contained weather system — wind pouring out of its north face, sweeping around, and returning into its south, in closed loops without beginning or end (which is *why* you can't cut off "just the wind's source": halve the system and you get two smaller complete weather systems). Earth is the grandest of these systems, its wind streaming pole to pole across ten thousand miles — and a compass is simply a weathervane for it.

## Mini Summary

- ✔ Like poles repel, unlike attract; poles come only in pairs — forever
- ✔ Field lines: N → S outside, denser = stronger, never crossing; filings and compasses read them
- ✔ Ferromagnetic club: iron, nickel, cobalt (+ alloys); domains align to magnetise
- ✔ Conductor ≠ magnetic: copper conducts and ignores; steel does both
- ✔ Earth's churning iron core makes a planetary field — compass, auroras, and shield

# Integration

**Earth Science**: Earth's field is geophysics' window into the deep interior — its generation in the molten outer core, its recorded reversals striping the mid-ocean seafloor (the smoking-gun evidence for plate tectonics), and its ongoing weakening-and-wander, monitored by satellite. The auroras are its visible collision with the solar wind.

**History**: The compass reached Europe via Arab traders from Song-dynasty China and rewired civilisation — enabling open-sea navigation, the age of exploration, and global trade centuries before anyone could explain the needle. Gilbert's *De Magnete* (1600) — arguing Earth itself is the magnet — is often called the first modern experimental physics book; the Tower's lodestone tradition descends from exactly that lineage.
